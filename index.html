anti-pangram

A structural pattern checker for your own drafts — not a Pangram clone, not a predictor of its score.

What this actually does: Pangram's real detection model is proprietary and unreachable from outside — nothing here can reconstruct its actual reasoning. What it can do is flag a specific, well-documented pattern: paragraphs that read as mechanically uniform (often the ones synthesizing a bullet list, or written in tightly parallel technical prose). That structural uniformity is one of the most literature-backed signals AI detectors key on, so fixing it is a reasonable, honest way to reduce your real false-positive rate — even though it can't guarantee any specific Pangram verdict.

What gets checked, and why
  • Follows a bulleted/numbered list — paragraphs that immediately restate a preceding list in prose are the single most common false-positive trigger in technical writing.
  • Uniform sentence length — low variance in words-per-sentence (low "burstiness") across a paragraph, a classic statistical AI-text signal.
  • Repeated sentence openers — two or more sentences in the same paragraph starting with the same word ("This…", "This…"), a common LLM tic.
  • Nominalizations — verbs turned into abstract nouns ("revise" → "revision process", "interpret" → "interpretation"). In one documented adversarial test against Pangram, this was the single strongest lever for pushing a rating from Human toward AI.
  • Polysyllabic word ratio — a high share of three-plus-syllable words in a paragraph, another lever from that same test. Flagged gently, since technical writing legitimately runs higher on this than casual prose — treat this one as the most likely false alarm of the set.
  • Passive voice — two or more passive constructions ("was flagged", "is handled") in one paragraph, reading more like a report than a person narrating events.
  • Generic connective phrases — stock transitions ("Together, these…", "It's worth noting…", "delve into", "complex tapestry"). Weighted lightly on its own: an adversarial test against Pangram found that swapping a "suspicious" word for a plainer one sometimes moved a passage toward an AI rating, not away — there's no reliable AI-word blacklist, so this signal only matters in combination with the others.
  • Low concrete specificity — no numbers, proper nouns, contractions, or first-person markers anywhere in the paragraph — i.e. nothing a generic model couldn't have written about any topic.
  • Em dashes, triads, "not just X but Y", markdown formatting, emoji — five checks sourced directly from Pangram's own published evidence guide, which lists each as several times more common in AI text than human text (e.g. em dashes ~10x, markdown ~12x, triads ~4x). These are illustrative signals Pangram shows users, not literal inputs to its model, but they're the closest thing to Pangram's own words on what it considers evidence.

Severity is a simple weighted count of these signals, shown as high/medium so you know where to spend editing time first — it is not a probability and is not calibrated against Pangram's own scoring. Detectors like Pangram reportedly operate on hard internal thresholds, so a single small edit near that boundary can flip a verdict even though the underlying text barely changed — a paragraph still being flagged after one fix doesn't necessarily mean the fix did nothing.

The "3 variations" per flagged paragraph are generated mechanically — reordering your existing sentences, merging two with a semicolon, or applying literal fixes (stripping a leading connector, swapping a nominalization for its verb form, converting a clear passive-with-"by" sentence to active voice) — never inventing new wording. When a paragraph is too short or simple for 3 safe mechanical moves, fewer are shown rather than padding with a fake one. Always proofread a variation before using it — mechanical reordering can occasionally read awkwardly even when grammatically valid.

Uploading a Pangram report PDF extracts each colored segment Pangram itself labeled (Human Written / AI Assisted / Fully AI Generated, with its confidence level), entirely in your browser via a locally-bundled copy of Mozilla's pdf.js — the PDF is never uploaded anywhere. Each segment is then run through the same structural checks above, so you can see where Pangram's own verdict and this tool's structural evidence agree or disagree. Parsing is based on Pangram's current report layout and may break if they change its format — if a PDF doesn't parse, the extracted text still lands in the box above for you to check and analyze manually. If a page has no real text layer (Pangram's report export is a rasterized image, not real text), it's run through a locally-bundled copy of Tesseract.js OCR instead — also entirely in-browser, nothing uploaded — which is generally accurate but not perfect (it reliably misreads "AI" as "Al", which is corrected automatically; other misreads may slip through, so proofread OCR'd text before trusting the analysis).
